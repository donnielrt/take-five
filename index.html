II

The Interpretation

FIVE is an original performance and visual piece in the spirit of Take Five — not a copy of the recording. The number five is made visible: a five-armed mandala counts the meter, the sax melody spirals outward (height = pitch), and color follows the harmony — cool blue at rest, gold on the dominant, resolving back to blue.

What you’re seeing

  • The five-armed mandala counts 1→2 →3→4→5 every bar — the 5/4 made visible.
  • The melodic spiral is the sax: higher pitch sits farther from the center, and notes fade over ~5 seconds.
  • Color is harmony — blue when the music is at home, gold on the dominant (tension), back to blue on resolve.
  • Inner rings are the walking bass; the glow pulses with the beat; the smoke is just the lounge.

I built FIVE to understand why Take Five feels the way it does — and to say that feeling back in its own voice. The whole work rests on one idea: make the meter visible. At the center of the screen is “now”; everything radiates outward from the present moment.

The arc

Title / count-in — “FIVE” fades in as five taps establish the meter. The head — the syncopated riff and its answer; blue → gold on the dominant → back to blue. The solo — denser, legato; the spiral fills and winds richer. The tag — a rising flourish, and the whole five-fold star ignites. The resolve — back to blue, and a fade to black as the reverb rings out.

The music

An original arrangement in Bb, 5/4 (felt as 2+3). One chord per bar over an eight-bar form: Bb mMaj7 · Cm9 · Gm9 · F7 · Bbmaj7 · Eb9 · Cm7 · Bb mMaj7. Alto sax, walking upright bass, Rhodes-style comping, and a swing ride — all synthesized from a deterministic score.

III

The Process

How it came together — the thinking, the decisions, and the raw material. If you play music, the most interesting file here is probably the score: the actual notes, chords, and timings.

The brief. Make an original, genuinely enthralling visual interpretation of Take Five — not a copy. That constraint became the creative engine: I couldn’t lean on the recording, so I had to find what the piece is and say it in a new voice.

The idea. The whole piece orbits one question — how do you make 5/4 visible? We feel five beats in the body, but we can’t see them. So: a five-armed mandala that counts 1–2–3–4–5 every bar. The meter becomes a shape you watch, and the oddness stops feeling like a bug and starts feeling like a feature.

The music. I wrote an original arrangement in the spirit of the piece — 5/4, key of Bb, a syncopated head, the minor–major-7 “Brubeck” harmony, a walking bass, a lyrical sax solo. It’s synthesized by code (alto sax, upright bass, Rhodes-style comping, swing ride) and never touches the copyrighted recording.

The visuals. One rule: the screen is a pure function of time. The sax melody spirals (height = pitch); color follows the harmony (blue = home, gold = the dominant’s tension, back to blue on resolve); the bass is a heartbeat near the core; smoke is just the lounge.

The build. Everything hangs off one deterministic score — the notes, chords, and timings — so sound and picture lock to every note. A single Canvas-2D renderer draws it identically in the browser, in a CPU preview, and in the rendered video. The audio is synthesized two ways (Web Audio in the browser, a pure-Node engine for the video) from that same score.

A constraint that shaped it. I built this inside a sandbox with no GPU and no working headless browser, so the video was rendered on the CPU (node-canvas → ffmpeg). Slower — but it guaranteed the video is pixel-identical to what plays live in a browser.

The finish. An opening title, and a single climactic flare — the whole five-fold star igniting on the final note — then a fade to black.

Decisions, and why

Why make the meter the star?
5/4 is the whole identity of Take Five, and it’s the one thing you can’t see in a normal music video. Making it the centerpiece is the most honest visual idea.
Why an original composition, not the recording?
The 1959 recording and the exact notation are copyrighted. More than that, inventing in its spirit is a more interesting creative act than re-skinning the existing record — and it’s what makes this mine to show you.
Why ~48 seconds, not 7:25?
The real song’s length is mostly improvised solos. This is a composed, self-contained statement — a beginning, middle, and end. A visualization holds attention best as one focused arc. (A full-length version is on the table for later.)
Why Canvas 2D, not WebGL?
One pure renderer that runs identically everywhere beats a flashier 3D scene that can’t be reproduced frame-for-frame. Browsers GPU-composite Canvas 2D anyway, so it still lives on the graphics card.
Why gold on the dominant?
In jazz, the dominant chord is the “pull” that begs to resolve. Giving it the one warm color makes tension-and-release something you can see, not just hear.
Why does it look the same in the video and the browser?
Because both are the same function of the same score. That parity was a hard requirement — the video isn’t a “render”; it’s the piece, frozen in time.
